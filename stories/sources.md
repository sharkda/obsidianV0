Good instinct to check first. The short version: **stories in the public domain are safe to read — but the single biggest trap is the translation.**

**What's IP-free (public domain) in Taiwan**

Taiwan's copyright term is life of the author + 50 years. Once that passes, the _work_ is free to use. That covers a huge amount of great story material:

- **Chinese classics** — 西遊記, 三國演義, 水滸傳, 紅樓夢, 聊齋誌異, plus 成語故事 and 寓言. Centuries old, unquestionably public domain.
- **Western fairy tales** — Grimm (格林童話), Andersen (安徒生), Aesop's fables (伊索寓言), Perrault. All long expired.
- **Classic myths & folklore** — Greek/Roman myths, 民間故事, 神話傳說.
- **Older Western literature** — anything by authors who died before ~1975 (e.g., Shakespeare, Dickens, Jules Verne, and many more).

**The catch that actually causes legal trouble**

The original story being public domain does **not** make a _modern Chinese version_ free. Three things carry their own fresh copyright:

- **Translations** — A recent 中文 translation of Grimm is the translator's copyrighted work, protected for their life + 50 years. Reading a specific published translation aloud = infringement, even though Grimm himself is free.
- **Adaptations / retellings** — 改編 or 橋梁書 versions (simplified, modernized, "retold by...") are new copyrighted works.
- **Specific editions & illustrations** — the particular book's text arrangement and art are protected.

**How to stay completely safe**

The cleanest path is to **read from the original public-domain text, or write your own retelling in your own words.** For Chinese classics this is easy — the classical source text is free. For foreign tales, either find a public-domain translation (older ones whose translator also died 50+ years ago) or, better, **read the story, then tell it in your own words.** Your own retelling becomes _your_ copyright — no trouble, and it's more natural for a kid audience anyway.

Penalties in Taiwan aren't trivial (unauthorized reproduction can mean up to 3 years imprisonment and a fine up to NT$750,000), so the caution is warranted — but the fix is simple: **own text or original-source text only.**

One more free, safe source: the **文化部兒童文化館** and other government-published story collections are often openly licensed for exactly this kind of use — worth checking their terms.

I'm not a lawyer and this isn't legal advice — for anything you plan to monetize, a quick check with a Taiwan IP professional is worth it. But for reading classics or your own retellings, you're on very solid ground.

**Best free sources for the original text (繁體):**

**One important caution for your podcast:**

- The **文言文 original** = public domain, safe. ✅

- **嶗山道士** — A lazy man learns to walk through walls from a mountain sage, but his bad character makes the magic fail and he bonks his head. Funny, with a clear moral.

- **罵鴨** — A man steals and eats a neighbor's duck, then sprouts duck feathers all over his body; the only cure is to confess. Hilarious honesty lesson.
- **蛙曲 / 鼠戲** — Two tiny sketches: a man who trains frogs to "sing" in tune, and performing mice that act out little plays. Whimsical and very short — good filler episodes.

**Great stories, with a small caveat to preview first:**

- **大鼠** — A palace cat repeatedly retreats from a monstrous rat, tiring it out, then wins by cleverness rather than force. Strategy over strength. (Mild animal fight.)
- **狼三則** — A butcher outwits a pack of wolves on a dark road. Exciting and taught in schools — but it does involve killing the wolves, so preview for tone.
